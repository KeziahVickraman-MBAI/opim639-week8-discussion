Circle 1: Marketplaces · the figure places it high cross-side, low same-side

Marketplaces are right on cross-side, but a “low” same-side score hides that sellers compete and buyers congest

◐ Half rightCross-side is high, but same-side is not low, it is often negative

Cross-side is correctly high

Riders want more drivers and drivers want more riders. The lecture’s Uber loop: more drivers, better coverage, shorter waits and lower prices, more riders. Airbnb, eBay and Grubhub work the same way.

More sellers means more competition

The lecture’s eBay slide says it directly: more sellers create more competition. More drivers in one zone means fewer trips each; more hosts in one district means lower occupancy. That is a negative same-side effect, not a missing one.

More buyers at once means congestion

Many riders at the same time trigger surge pricing and longer waits. A dinner rush slows deliveries. Congestion is the lecture’s example of a negative externality.

The chart has no minus sign

The axes run only from low to high, so a strong negative effect and no effect land in the same place. For marketplaces that sign drives fees, pricing and supplier churn: Homejoy’s 25% cut plus competition pushed experienced cleaners out.

Cross-side creates a tipping point

Because each side’s value depends on the other, a marketplace must pass a critical mass first. SideCar had good technology but never got enough drivers and riders. Platforms subsidise one side, such as driver bonuses and low introductory prices, to get over the hump.

● INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERAdd drivers to one zone

Fixed demand of 120 ride requests an hour in one zone. Each driver can complete at most 2.5 trips an hour, at an average fare of $12.

Our conclusion

Partly correct. Marketplaces belong high on cross-side, but same-side is real and mostly negative: suppliers compete and buyers congest at peaks.

A better chart would let the same-side axis go below zero. That is why marketplaces manage supply balance, fees and quality, not just growth.

SPEAKER NOTES

Agree with the vertical position first. The lecture's two-sided loop is exactly this circle: more buyers attract more sellers, more sellers attract more spending, and the Uber example shows coverage and price improving as drivers join. Then challenge the horizontal position. The lecture's own eBay slide marks same-side effects on both sides, including a minus sign for sellers: more sellers create more competition. Drivers in the same zone compete for the same riders, and hosts in the same district compete for the same guests. On the buyer side, peaks create congestion, which the lecture uses as its example of a negative externality. The figure's axis only measures size, so it shows these as “low”. That matters because the negative side is where marketplaces fail: Homejoy's 25% cut and fierce competition pushed good cleaners away, and customers who found a good cleaner went direct. Finally, the high cross-side effect is what creates the tipping point from the network effects model. Below the critical mass z′ the market shrinks to zero, which is what happened to SideCar. Lowering prices or subsidising one side moves that tipping point left and is why marketplaces spend heavily early on. Overall verdict for the figure: a useful rough map, not correct in detail.

Circle 2: Communications Networks · the figure places it high same-side, low cross-side

Messaging apps do belong high on same-side, but the value comes from your own contacts, not the size of the network

✓ Mostly rightSame-side is high, but it is local and easy to share, and cross-side is rising

High same-side is correct

A messaging app is worthless alone and more useful with every contact who joins. It is the lecture’s positive externality in its purest form: f(z) does almost all the work.

But the effect is local, not global

I gain when my 30 contacts join, not when a stranger in another country does. Billions of WhatsApp users matter little to me if my friends are on LINE or WeChat.

Multi-homing weakens the lock-in

A second chat app is free and takes minutes to install, so many people use three or four. Hangouts had Google’s huge user base behind it and was still shut down.

Cross-side is no longer low

WhatsApp and Messenger now charge businesses to message customers, and Snapchat sells advertisers access to its users. Businesses join because users are there: a cross-side effect.

Critical mass is won group by group

The tipping point applies to each friend group, team or class, not to a whole country. That is why GroupMe spread through teams and classes, and why a whole group can leave together.

● INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERYour contacts, not total users

You have 20 close contacts. Move the sliders to see where your value from a messaging app really comes from.

Our conclusion

Mostly correct. Communications networks belong high on same-side and still fairly low on cross-side.

But the same-side effect is local and multi-homing is cheap, so it protects less than the chart suggests, and a paying business side is growing. A size-only axis misses both.

SPEAKER NOTES

This is the circle the figure gets most right, so say so first. Messaging is the cleanest case of the lecture's positive externality: each new user raises the value for existing users, and nobody pays anybody for it. In the model, f(z) dominates and intrinsic interest r(x) is small, because a chat app on its own does almost nothing. Then add two refinements. First, z is not the total number of users, it is the number of people I actually talk to. So the effect is strong but local, which is why WhatsApp, LINE and WeChat each dominate different countries instead of one app winning everywhere. Second, switching and multi-homing are cheap. Homejoy showed in class how easy switching hurts a platform; for messaging it means a big network can still lose, as Hangouts did. On the vertical axis, the figure was fair when these apps were purely person to person, but WhatsApp and Messenger now sell business messaging and Snapchat sells advertising, so a second side is growing. Finally, the tipping point from the model happens inside each group: once the core of a class or team joins, the rest follow, and lowering the price to zero is what makes that first step easy. Verdict: right on direction, but the axis overstates how protective that same-side effect is.
