Marketplaces 1 of 3: cross-side · the figure places it high cross-side, low same-side

The figure is right that marketplaces live on cross-side effects, and that is why they must pass a tipping point first

✓ CorrectCross-side is high, and it is what creates the tipping point

Cross-side is correctly high

Riders want more drivers and drivers want more riders. The lecture’s Uber loop: more drivers, better coverage, shorter waits and lower prices, more riders. Airbnb, eBay and Grubhub work the same way.

Each side’s value depends on the other

A user joins if r(x)·f(z) ≥ p: intrinsic interest times the benefit from the other side. With no drivers, f(0) = 0, so nobody pays, however much they want a ride.

That creates a tipping point

Below the critical mass z′, users leave and the market shrinks to zero. Above it, each new user attracts more until the market settles at z″.

SideCar never got over the hump

SideCar had good technology but never got enough drivers and riders. Riders waited, left for rivals, and drivers followed them.

Subsidies move the tipping point

Driver bonuses and low introductory prices move z′ left, so it is easier to pass, and z″ right, so the final market is bigger. That is why marketplaces spend heavily early on.

● INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERFind the tipping point

The lecture’s model: user x joins if r(x)·f(z) ≥ p, with r(x) = 1 − x and f(z) = z. The curve is what the next user will pay once a share z has joined.

Our conclusion

Correct on cross-side. Marketplaces belong high on the vertical axis.

That strength cuts both ways: it is why a marketplace can grow fast once it passes critical mass, and why it dies if it does not. Early subsidies are the price of getting over the hump.

SPEAKER NOTES

Agree with the vertical position first. The lecture's two-sided loop is exactly this circle: more buyers attract more sellers, more sellers attract more spending, and the Uber example shows coverage and price improving as drivers join. Then connect it to the network effects model. A user buys if r(x) times f(z) is at least the price. With r(x) = 1 − x and f(z) = z there are three equilibria: zero, the unstable tipping point z′ and the stable market z″. Use the explainer: at the full price, launching with 20% of users is below z′ and the market collapses, which is the SideCar story. Switch to the introductory price: z′ moves left, the same launch is now above it, and the market grows to a bigger z″. That is the lecture's argument for free trials and low introductory prices, and why Uber and Grab paid driver bonuses and subsidised rides in new cities. So the figure's high cross-side score is right, and it explains both the growth and the fragility of marketplaces.

Marketplaces 2 of 3: same-side · the figure places it low same-side

A “low” same-side score hides that sellers compete and buyers congest

✕ Not correctSame-side is not low, it is often negative

More sellers means more competition

The lecture’s eBay slide says it directly: more sellers create more competition. More drivers in one zone means fewer trips each; more hosts in one district means lower occupancy. That is a negative same-side effect, not a missing one.

More buyers at once means congestion

Many riders at the same time trigger surge pricing and longer waits. A dinner rush slows deliveries. Congestion is the lecture’s example of a negative externality.

The chart has no minus sign

The axes run only from low to high, so a strong negative effect and no effect land in the same place. For marketplaces that sign drives fees, pricing and supplier churn: Homejoy’s 25% cut plus competition pushed experienced cleaners out.

Some same-side effects are positive

On eBay more buyers make the platform better, and more reviews on Airbnb help other guests choose. The sign depends on the side and the moment, which one number cannot show.

So marketplaces manage balance, not just growth

Surge pricing, zone bonuses, fees and quality checks exist to stop one side crowding itself. Growth alone makes the squeeze worse.

● INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERAdd drivers to one zone

Fixed demand of 120 ride requests an hour in one zone. Each driver can complete at most 2.5 trips an hour, at an average fare of $12.

Our conclusion

Not correct on same-side. It is real and mostly negative: suppliers compete and buyers congest at peaks.

A better chart would let the same-side axis go below zero. Overall the Marketplaces circle is half right: high cross-side, but not low same-side.

Now challenge the horizontal position. The lecture's own eBay slide marks same-side effects on both sides, including a minus sign for sellers: more sellers create more competition. Drivers in the same zone compete for the same riders, and hosts in the same district compete for the same guests. Use the explainer: up to about 48 drivers, every extra driver helps riders and earns fully. Past that point riders gain little, and each extra driver cuts everyone's earnings. Switch the cut to 25% and earnings drop further, which is the Homejoy problem: its 25% cut and fierce competition pushed good cleaners away, and customers who found a good cleaner went direct. On the buyer side, peaks create congestion, which the lecture uses as its example of a negative externality. Some same-side effects are positive, like more buyers and reviews on eBay, so the true sign varies. The figure's axis only measures size, so it shows all of this as "low". Overall verdict for the Marketplaces circle: half right.

Marketplaces 3 of 3: online marketplaces · the figure’s seven companies on a signed axis

Give the same-side axis a minus sign and the Marketplaces circle splits apart

◐ Half rightThe circle’s height is right; its spot on same-side differs by business

The figure scores size, not sign

A “low” same-side score puts no effect and a strong negative effect in the same place. Our chart runs from negative through zero to positive instead.

Ride-hailing and delivery sit left of zero

Uber, Lyft and Grubhub: suppliers compete for the same jobs and buyers crowd the same peak hours. More of the same side makes things worse.

Dating apps are crowded on each side

On Tinder, users compete with others like them for the same matches. The value comes from the other side; more people like you mostly adds competition.

Reviews pull some to the right

On Amazon and eBay, every extra buyer adds reviews, ratings and purchase data that help other buyers choose. The lecture’s point on lower search costs works on the same side here.

Airbnb is mixed

Hosts in one district compete for the same guests, but more guest reviews help future guests. The sign depends on which side and which city.

● INTERACTIVE EXPLAINERWhere we would actually place them

The figure scores size only, so all seven share one “low” circle, on the positive side of zero.

With a minus sign, five of the seven move left of zero. Only Amazon, and eBay just about, gain from more of the same side.

Our conclusion

Half right: the circle’s height is right, its horizontal spot is not.

On a signed axis the seven spread from clearly negative (Uber, Lyft) to mildly positive (Amazon). One circle hides that the same-side effect depends on the business model.
